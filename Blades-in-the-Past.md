
# The Basics

## Touchstones

* This is based on the *Blades in the Dark* SRD. Which is the distilled system from *Blades in the Dark* by John Harper. Most of the mechancs of this game derive from that game.
* The main flavour and setting touchstone for this game is the 1999 RPG *CºNTINUUM: Roleplaying in the Yet* by Chris Adams, Dave Fooden, and Barbara Manui, which inspired the implied setting and some of the mechanics. Although very hard to obtain a copy in the current era it is highly recommended.
* **Scum and Villainy** by Stras Acimovic and John LeBoeuf-Little is another game derived from the Forged in the Dark system, which has helped give ideas for how to adapt the system to a very different setting.
* Additional inspiration was taken from **GURPS Time Travel** by Steve Jackson and John M. Ford.
* Additional inspiration was also taken from **TIMEWATCH** by Kevin Kulp.

## What is a Roleplaying Game

This should probably not be your first Roleplaying Game. There are many games which are excellent for introducing new players, but this was not a design goal for this game. I would suggest that **Blades in the Dark** or **Scum and Villainy** would make better starting points.

## Core Conceit

You are a group of daring time travellers granted a level of initiation into the ranks of a time spanning society of time travellers. You each have been given the ability to travel through space-time *at will* with only a few limits. You can instantly and nearly effortlessly move from one moment to another without passing through the intervening space-time, and whilst there are limits to how much you can do this between rests they are fairly generous.

Within the time travelling culture to which you have been inducted there are large fraternal societies dedicated to particular aims and goals when using these abilities, and your group are pledged to one of these fraternal orders. What you do with your time jumping capability will depend strongly on which of these you are pledged to.

However, there is one unbreakable rule of time travel:

**What has been seen to occur MUST occur**

No matter how powerful and godlike you may feel your time jumping makes you it will always be impossible for you to change the known past. Changes can occur only in the places where the outcome is not known.


But don't despair! The historical record is full of holes, and sometimes what you have seen happen is not what you *think* you have seen happen...

## Record Keeping

This is a game which involves more record keeping than most RPGs. Every effort has been made to simplify the record keeping and to keep it focused only on what matters, but this is not the kind of game where you can easily get away with playing fast and loose with the historical record.

That's just the nature of maintaining continuity, and the GM especially is responsible for making sure continuity is preserved.

That said, the game has been written to promote improvisation and avoid the need for detailed planning ahead. Its just that when you improvise, you need to record what you improvised.

# The Core System

## Judgment calls

When you play, you'll make several key judgment calls. Everyone contributes, but either the players or the GM gets final say for each:

* Which actions are reasonable as a solution to a problem? *Can this person be swayed? Must we get out the tools and tinker with this old rusty lock, or could it also be quietly finessed?* The players have final say.
* How dangerous and how effective is a given action in this circumstance? *How risky is this? Can this person be swayed very little or a whole lot?* The GM has final say.
* Which consequences are inflicted to manifest the dangers in a given circumstance? *Does this fall from the roof break your leg? Do the local cops merely become suspicious or do they see something they shouldn't?* The GM has final say.
* Does this situation call for a dice roll, and which one? *Is your character in position to make an action roll or must they first make a resistance roll to gain initiative?* The GM has final say.

## Rolling the Dice

*Blades in the Past* uses six-sided dice. You roll several at once and read the **single highest result**.

* If the highest die is a **6**, it's a **full success**—things go well. If you roll more than one **6**, it's a **critical success**—you gain some additional advantage.
* If the highest die is a **4 or 5**, that's a **partial success**—you do what you were trying to do, but there are consequences: trouble, harm, reduced effect, etc.
* If the highest die is **1-3**, it's a **bad outcome**. Things go poorly. You probably don't achieve your goal and you suffer complications, too.

*If you ever need to roll but you have zero (or negative) dice, roll two dice and take the single lowest result. You can't roll a **critical** when you have zero dice.*

All the dice systems in the game are expressions of this basic format. When you're first learning the game, you can always "collapse" back down to a simple roll to judge how things go. Look up the exact rule later when you have time.

To create a dice pool for a roll, you'll use a **trait** (like your **Finesse** or your **Self** or your circle's rank) and take dice equal to its **rating**. You'll usually end up with one to four dice. Even one die is pretty good in this game—a 50% chance of success. The most common traits you'll use are the **action ratings** of the player characters. A player might roll dice for their **Fight** action rating when they fight an enemy, for example.

There are four types of rolls that you'll use most often in the game:

* **Action roll**. When a PC attempts an action that's dangerous or troublesome, you make an action roll to find out how it goes. Action rolls and their effects and consequences drive most of the game.
* **Downtime roll**. When the PCs are killing time (see later), they can perform downtime activities in relative safety. You make downtime rolls to see how much they get done.
* **Fortune roll**. The GM can make a fortune roll to disclaim decision making and leave something up to chance. *How loyal is an NPC? How much does the plague spread? How much is burned before the jumpers jumped in?*
* **Resistance roll**. A player can make a resistance roll when their character suffers a consequence they don't like. The roll tells us how much paradox their character suffers to reduce the severity of a consequence. *When you resist that "Broken Leg" harm, you take some paradox and now it's only a "Sprained Ankle" instead. You landed on something soft, better make sure you remember to put that there.*


## Game Structure

This game has a structure built around two phases of play:

* **Free Play**: in which players roleplay their characters, the narrative is the strongest driver, and mechanics are use sparingly and as appropriate.
* **Escapades**: in which play focuses in on the mechanical aspects of the game as the PCs attempt to achieve a specific goal and deel with complications which arise as a result.

These two phases alternate, as we switch from free play to an escapade and then back to free play again.

A third "pseudo-phase" exists, **Killing Time**, in which a slightly different set of mechanics are used to measure long term actions and projects which take extended periods of time to complete. But this is usually not an entire phase in of itself, rather characters can activate the killing time rules during Free Play and Escapades to engage in long term projects as needed. This is one of the advantages of being able to time travel.

# The Mechanics of Space-Time Travel

All PCs in this game are **jumpers**, people who have been given the ability to travel in space-time at will. What is more all PCs are, at least initially, inducted members in good standing of the overarching society of jumpers which maintains general control of jumpers within most of human space-time. Jumpers who are not members in good standing do exist, and occasionally even grant the gift of jumping to others without inducting them into the society. Such people are usually highly dangerous and do not handle their newfound gifts well.

## Moments

At every point in game-play every PC is located at a specified **moment** in space-time. Each moment consists of a time and a place. Usuaully specified with a date and a general location. There is no hard boundary to how large a moment is in both time and space, rather it is a game-play abstraction designed to make space-time travel easier to deal with. As a general rule of thumb two characters are occupying the same moment if they are able to easily interact with each other due to proximity in space at the same point in time.

During an operations it's important to keep track of what moments every character has or will visit. It can be useful to have some file cards or pieces of paper aranged in a rough timeline on the table representing general periods of interest in the current timeline, and note down on them the times each character arrives and leaves them.

For the purpose of this game all dates and times are identified in the following format:

YYYY-MM-DD hh:mm:ssZ

Where YYYY is the four-digit year, MM is the two digit month, DD is the two digit day, hh is the two digit hour in 24-hour clock, mm is the two digit minutes, and ss is the two digit seconds. The suffic Z means that this is in the UTC timezone, and in this text that timezone will generally be used for all times unless specified otherwise. It's usually a good idea to keep track of which timezone the majority of action in your game will take place in and use that.

## Logbook

Every character needs to maintain a logbook. This goes along with the character's character sheet. It contains two sections: one for recording events in your personal past, and one for recording your destiny.

You may use a special sheet for your logbook, but a simple notebook can also work well. The following are the usual circumstances under which you need to make entries in your logbook:

* Whenever you depart from a particular moment by using a jumper action you will note the moment of departure, including date, time, and location.
* Whenever you arrive in a particular moment using a jumper action you will note the moment of arrival including date, time, and location.
* Whenever you **Kill Time** you will note the start and end moments (with date, time, and location) as well as a quick summary of any **downtime actions** taken during the interval (see downtime section).

## Jump Points

Every jumper has 50 Jump Points which can be spent and regained performing the jumper actions listed below. In free play we usually don't track this closely but instead just allow jumping within the limits described below. During Escapades these Jump Points are a carefully managed resource, and one of the primary ones for the game.

### Exhaustion

When a jumper has 0JP left they are considered **exhausted**. When a character is exhausted all actions which would usually cost JP *even jumping spatially which costs 0JP* instead cause the character to take **harm**. The level of harm is equal to the cost in JP, with a minimum of level 1 harm.

## Paradox

Every character has a 9 segment long **Paradox Track** on their character sheet. Taking points of **paradox** occurs when you take actions which appear to risk altering the known past. For every point of paradox you gain you will record an item in your **destiny** which specifies how the paradox can be removed. When the destiny item is fulfilled you lose a number of points of paradox equal to the number you gained when it was created.

When the paradox track fills up mark a single point of **distortion** instead. Unlike paradox distortion cannot be removed at all easily, and has permanent lasting effects. You are no longer completely in sync with the flow of time.

Whenever you mark a point of **distortion** take a new condition from the following list:

* **Translucent** -- you do not appear wholy there to the naked eye
* **Delayed** -- there is a subtle timing difference between your actions and their effects
* **Delocalised** -- you no longer have a single location in space, but seem to occupy several
* **Repetative** -- you are prone to repeating the same phrases or actions over and over
* **Ephemeral** -- you can no longer easily interract with physical objects

### Entanglement

Some effects cause entanglment. When a character becomes entangled their ability to recharge spent JP is severely curtailed. The most common cause of entanglement is when one character takes an action to cause another to take paradox. At that point both characters become entangled. The entanglement ends when either one character has caused *two* points of paradox to another through direct actions, or when all but one character can no longer achieve their goals in this time region.

See **time combat** later for more details.

## Initiation Rank

Every society jumper has an initiation rank within the society. This determines the limits of their time jumping capabilities. It is widely suspected by more junior members that the limits imposed on them are actually enforced restrictions rather than limits in their actual ability to jump, but higher ranking jumpers refuse to comment.

| Rank | Duration per JP | Longjump Duration | Distance  | Mass Limit |
|------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------|------------|
|   1  | 1 week          | 1 year            | 2 km      | 20 kg      |
|   2  | 10 weeks        | 1 decade          | 20 km     | 200 kg     |
|   3  | 2 years         | 1 century         | 200 km    | 2 tonnes   |
|   4  | 2 decades       | 1 millenium       | 2,000 km  | 20 tonnes  |
|   5  | 2 centuries     | 10 millenia       | 20,000 km | 200 tonnes |

Ranks above 5 probably exist, but most jumpers will never knowlingly encounter someone with that level of initiation.

It is worth noting that even with the limits shown above characters can cover large amounts of history if they choose to spend their own experienced time travelling. A longjump (described in more detail below) can be attempted roughly once per experienced day, so even a rank 1 character could cover over three centuries in a year if they really wanted to.

It is also worth noting that a Rank 3 jumper can easily carry a group of other people with them when they jump, allowing them to ferry a group of lower rank jumpers to the time period they need to be at with minimal effort.

## Jumper Actions

There are a number of actions which jumpers may take which interact with space-time. Each of these actions is described below:

### Jump Across / Jump Spatially (0JP)

Any jumper may at any time choose to *jump spatially*. This means altering their position in space without altering their position in time. Essentially instantaneous teleportation. Each PC has a specific spacial distance limit to how far they can jump in a single jump (for starting characters this is 2km). Such a jump usually costs no jump points.

*During free play or when in a safe stable position a character may make multiple sequential spatial jumps to get to a specific location without needing to specify every intervening location, as long as the GM is comfortable with the jumper being able to avoid complications during these brief moments in between.*

### Jump Forward / Jump Ahead (1+JP)

Any jumper may at any point choose to *jump forward*. This means moving their position in time towards the future. Each character will have a specified *jump duration* on their character sheet which specifies the maximum temporaral distance which can be covered with a single JP (for starting characters this is 1 week).

If the character is in a safe location and in stable position then they may spend more than one JP to cover a longer span of time. The cost is one additional JP per additional full *jump duration*.

When changing location in time the jumper may also change location in space at the same time for no extra cost, with the same limitations as covered under jumping spatially. In particular if in free play or a safe stable position then the jumper may chain time and space jumps together to get to the correct moment.

Eg. Hans is located in an office of the *Museu Nacional* in Rio de Janeiro at 2018-09-02 23:30:05Z (19:30:05 local), he realises that the part of the museum he is in is still too crowded for his purposes, so he jumps ahead by three hours to 2018-09-03 02:30:05Z (22:30:05 local) and spends 1JP, plus marks his departure and arrival times in his logbook, arriving just as the area he was planning to investigate is engulfed in flames...

Eg. Haruhi has lost sight of her quarry, the thief Zara she has been tracking across London. It is 1998-12-08 15:23:18Z and she knows she's going to need more information to work out where the girl went between now and New Year's Eve, when a witness saw her at a party in Battersea making out with an unknown young woman. Deciding to take a different tack she jumps ahead to just outside the front door of the flat at which the party occurs at 1998-12-31 23:50:14Z; That's a jump of between 3 and 4 weeks (3 weeks, 2 days, 8 hours, 26 minutes, and 56 seconds to be precise, but her player doesn't need to work that out) and several kilometers, spending 4JP for the jump she marks her departure and arrival in her logbook, the GM judges she doesn't need to worry about the sequential spatial jumps, most of which land on the flat roofs of towerblocks. She's now ready to talk her way into the party and see if she can convince the girl to give her some clues.

### Jump Backward / Jump Back (1+JP)

Any jumper may at any point choose to *jump backward*. This means moving their position in time towards the past. Each character will have a specified *jump duration* on their character sheet which specifies the maximum temporaral distance which can be covered with a single JP (for starting characters this is 1 week).

If the character is in a safe location and in stable position then they may spend more than one JP to cover a longer span of time. The cost is one additional JP per additional full *jump duration*.

When changing location in time the jumper may also change location in space at the same time for no extra cost, with the same limitations as covered under jumping spatially. In particular if in free play or a safe stable position then the jumper may chain time and space jumps together to get to the correct moment.

Eg. Hans is in danger from the encroaching flames! He skips back another hour, to 2018-09-03 01:30:05Z (21:30:05 local), spends 1JP, logs his departure and arrival, and finds himself staring at the back of a researcher who is desperately grabbing papers from the office before leaving. This is not going according to plan!

Eg. At 1999-01-01 02:55:18 Haruhi leaves the flat having spent several hours in Zara's company. She makes a personal note to call her next time she's in early January 1999. This isn't a destined event, though, just a thing she intends to do, so it doesn't need to be recorded in her destiny. Zara was bragging about getting a big payout from selling something to a fence in Wandsworth on Christmas Eve. Now Haruhi knows the fence in question, and decides to pay him a visit just after Zara left him (Zara didn't know her until New Year's Eve, meeting early could cause paradox), so she jumps back to his lockup on 1998-12-25 00:00:00Z, figuring he'll be at home for Christmas. This costs 2JP, and again the GM decides to permit multiple chained spatial jumps to cover the distance. Unfortunately she was wrong about the whereabouts of the Fence at that moment ...

### Longjump (50JP)

For an entire 50JP a jumper may jump either forward or back by their listed **longjump duration**. For starting characters that's 1 year. Since this always uses up 50JP it leaves the jumper **exhausted**. 

### Recharge (restores JP, interruptable, not whilst entangled)

When a character is in a safe location and a stable position then they may choose to recharge. This involves spending at least eight hours sleeping or otherwise fully resting. Move the character 8 hours forward at no cost and restore any spent JP. Record departure and arrival as you would for a jump, but note it was for recharging.

This action can be **interrupted** if someone takes actions targetting the character during the period when they are resting, in this case move the character forward only to the point of interruption and record that moment as your arrival in the logbook. Interrupted recharging does not usually restore JP, though at GM's discretion it may be permissible to resume the recharge after the interruption and get the full effect.

This action cannot be used if the character is currently **entangled**.

### Extended time travel (special, interruptable, free play only)

During free play a character may wish to spend an extended duration of their personal timeline travelling. This involves a series of longjumps alternating with the recharge action. In this case each longjump recharge pair takes 1 day, and the intermittant moments should be recorded in the logbook. At the end of the action record any increase in age rounded to the closest number of weeks.

Characters with rank 2 and above may be able to carry enough mass during a jump to carry another jumper, this allows a form of "relay" effect where two (or more) jumpers share the cost of longjumping during extended travel. That can essentially allow the group to make more than one longjump in a single experienced day.

At even higher ranks an experienced jumper can transport a group of less experienced jumpers to a moment they would otherwise have had trouble even reaching. For example a single rank 3 jumper can transport an entire circle up to a century in a single personal day.

### Declare a Detail (1+P)

For a cost of 1 paradox a jumper may, at any time, declare a small detail to be true as long as it does not directly contradict established facts. When you do this mark a new entry in your **destiny** to arrange that the detail is true, this is the condition for removing the paradox.

Eg. Haruhi needs to open a safe and remove the item inside then close the safe again during the small time frame between the fence leaving and him returning from the bathroom. To ensure she can do this as quickly as possible she spends 1 paradox to declare there is a piece of paper with the combination on it in the inside pocket of her jacket. To clear this paradox she's going to have to plant a copy of that note in her own pocket some time in her own personal past.

Some details may occasionally cost more than 1 paradox if they seem like they would be especially complex to arrange.

### Declare a Beneficial Situation (2P)

Just before a player makes an action roll they may declare a helpful situation which grants them +1d on the roll or +1 level of effect. This costs 2 paradox. Note down in your destiny that you must arrange this helpful situation at some point in your personal future, doing so is how you clear this paradox.

### Avoid Consequences (6 - roll P)

When a character is about to take a consquence of any sort the player can declare that they are avoiding it. For some consequences this will eliminate them entirely, for others this will reduce their severity by one level. In either case the player describes the circumstance which permits the avoidance and gets the benefit. Then they roll a pool of dice equal to their most apropriate attribute. Subtract the highest value of the roll from 6 to determine how much paradox is acrued.

As usual you must then note the circumstance which allowed the avoidance, and note in your destiny that you must arrange this, which is how you will clear the paradox.

### Double Trick (1P, limit 2 per escapade unless upgrade permits more)

An older version of yourself jumps into the current moment. This older self will act under the control of the GM, but the GM should take input from the player on what they would expect their older self to do in this situation.

Note in your destiny that you must travel to this moment and take these actions.

This can be a very dangerous choice, since it involves risking seeing your own future self suffer harm or other consequences, which you are then required to undergo if you ever want to clear your paradox.

### Killing Time (can be interrupted)

At any moment any character may choose to move themselves any duration forwards (and only forwards, you can't kill time backwards). This is done by *living sequentially without jumping*. This is the only time travel action non-jumpers can take, and they take it constantly.

When you kill time record it in your logbook just as you would for a jump departure. If you aren't **interrupted** then also record your arrival at the end of the duration, if you are interrupted record your arrival at the moment of interruption. Regardless of whether you were interrupted increase your age by the time between your departure and arrival, rounded down to a number of whole weeks.

If not **entangled** a jumper who is killing time for more than a day can **recharge** as well.

Killing time is an essential mechanic in this game because when you kill time you enter **downtime**, and may perform **downtime actions** using the systems described in the **downtime** section.

### Instant Downtime (1+JP, can be interrupted)

Instant downtime is a trick many jumpers use. It is a combination of chained jumps and killing time, but is called out here specifically because it is so commonly used.

To make use of instant downtime a jumper:

* **jumps** to a safe moment where they will have stable position
* **kills time** for a duration of their choice, making use of this time to perform **downtime actions**
* **jumps** to the moment from which they left

Usually one of these jumps will be **spatial** and the other a **jump backwards**, but on occasion it may be necessary to perform additional **jumps forward** or **jumps backwards** to get to the correct moment for performing the intended downtime actions.

When not entangled the killing time can be used to **recharge**, meaning that running out of JP is rarely a serious problem when not entangled.

When entangled this is much more tricky. Rank 1 characters particularly do not have a lot of scope to perform extended instant downtime actions when entangled.

### Reactive jump/Jumping dodge (0+JP, not when surprised)

When some physical harm is about to befall a jumper *and they can see it* they will usually respond by jumping away. Often this is a spatial jump, though it could be a jump ahead or behind too. Record the jump as usual and reduce the severity of the harm by one level.

This can be combined with **avoiding consequences** using paradox.

As a result of this it is usually not very effective to attack a jumper physically when they can see you.

## Special Jumping situations

* Strong electromagnetic of gravitational fields cost extra JP to cross, how many will vary on the strength of the field
* Strong radiation environments can have similar effects, and may even cause additional points of paradox.
* Some regions of space-time are so full of crossing worldlines from jumpers that they impose additional difficulties to jumping. The details of this are up to the GM, but usually involve added JP cost, and occasionally paradox or harm as well. These regions are very rare, and the GM should use them sparingly. Much more common, though still rare, are regions where entering causes *entanglement* in an ongoing temporal conflict.


# Actions, Atributes, and Skills

## Action Ratings

There are 12 **actions** in the game that the player characters use to overcome obstacles, each is associated with one of the three **atributes**:

* **Body**
  * **Drive**
  * **Fight**
  * **Run**
  * **Sneek**
* **Mind**
  * **Finesse**
  * **Understand**
  * **Study**
  * **Tinker**
* **Self**
  * **Attune**
  * **Command**
  * **Consort**
  * **Sway**

Each action has a rating (from zero to 4) that tells you how many dice to roll when you perform that action. Action ratings don't represent skill or training, but more of a general aptitude for an entire range of skills. 

## Action Roll

You make an **action roll** when your character does something potentially dangerous or troublesome. The possible results of the action roll depend on your character's **position**. There are three positions: **controlled**, **risky**, and **desperate**. If you're in a **controlled** position, the possible consequences are less serious. If you're in a **desperate** position, the consequences can be severe. If you're somewhere in between, it's **risky**—usually considered the "default" position for most actions.

If there's no danger or trouble at hand, you don't make an action roll. You might make a **fortune** roll or a **downtime** roll or the GM will simply say yes—and you accomplish your goal.

## Attribute Ratings

There are three **attributes** in the game system that the player characters use to resist bad consequences: **Body**, **Mind**, and **Self**. Each attribute has a rating (from zero to 4) that tells you how many dice to roll when you use that attribute.

The value of each attribute is the number of action ratings for that attribute which are non-zero.

## Skills

As well as **attributes** and **actions** every character has a list of **skills**. Skills are much more specific than actions, a character might have a rating of 2 in Tinker, but that doesn't make them equally adept at tinkering with all possible devices throughout space-time. A skill is an individual speciality which a PC has studied enough to use their actions to full effect with that thing.

Some example skills might include:
* Internal Combustion Engine Motorcars
* Semi-automatic weapons
* Middle Chinese Language
* 16th Century Mongol Court Etiquette
* 21st Century Financial Systems
* Lucid Dreaming
* Propeller aircraft
* Internet research

etc ...

A character begins play with 7 skills, but picking up more in play is extremely common. Skills don't have a rating, you either have them or you don't.

**When you attempt to take an action which requires a skill you don't have at all you position is automatically desperate and your effect cannot be more than limited**

**When you attempt to take an action where you have a similar but not perfect skill the GM can choose whether to restrict you to desperate position or limited effect, but perhaps not both**

# Progress clocks

A **progress clock** is a circle divided into segments. Draw a progress clock when you need to track ongoing effort against an obstacle or the approach of impending trouble.

*Sneaking into a watch tower? Make a clock to track the alert level of the patrolling guards. When the PCs suffer consequences from partial successes or missed rolls, fill in segments on the clock until the alarm is raised.*

Generally, the more complex the problem, the more segments in the progress clock.

A complex obstacle is a 4-segment clock. A more complicated obstacle is a 6-clock. A daunting obstacle is an 8-segment clock.

When you create a clock, make it about the **obstacle,** not the method. The clocks for an infiltration should be "Interior Patrols" not "Sneak Past the Guards". The patrols are the obstacle - the PCs can attempt to overcome them in a variety of ways.

Complex enemy threats can be broken into several "layers," each with its own progress clock. For example, a gangs' HQ might have a "Perimeter Security" clock, an "Interior Guards" clock, and a "Office Security" clock. The circle would have to make their way through all three layers to reach the gang boss' personal safe and valuables within.

Remember that a clock tracks progress. It reflects the fictional situation, so the group can gauge how they're doing. A clock is like a speedometer in a car. It *shows* the speed of the vehicle—it doesn't determine the speed.

## Simple Obstacles

Not every situation and obstacle requires a clock. Use clocks when a situation is complex or layered and you need to track something over time—otherwise, resolve the result of an action with a single roll.

Examples of progress clocks follow.

## Danger Clocks

The GM can use a clock to represent a progressive danger, like suspicion growing during a seduction, the proximity of pursuers in a chase, or the alert level of guards on patrol. In this case, when a complication occurs, the GM ticks one, two, or three segments on the clock, depending on the consequence level. When the clock is full, the danger comes to fruition—the guards hunt down the intruders, activate an alarm, release the hounds, etc.

## Racing Clocks

Create two opposed clocks to represent a race. The exhausted PCs might have a progress clock called "Find the artifact" while the GM has a clock called "Fire". If the PCs finish their clock before the GM fills theirs, they get to the artifact. Otherwise, they're cornered by spreading fire and may have to jump backwards to try a different approach. If both complete at the same time, the PCs reach the artifact just as the fire gets here!

## Linked Clocks

You can make a clock that unlocks another clock once it's filled. For example, when you fight a veteran warrior, she might have a clock for her "Defense" and then a linked clock for "Vulnerable." Once you overcome the "Defense" clock, then you can attempt to overcome the "Vulnerable" clock and defeat her.  You might affect the "Defense" clock with violence in a knife-fight, or you lower her defense with deception if you have the opportunity. As always, the method of action is up to the players and the details of the fiction at hand.

## Mission Clocks

The GM can make a clock for a mission, to represent the window of opportunity you have to complete it. If the countdown runs out, the mission is no longer viable as originally intended.

## Tug-of-war Clocks

You can make a clock that can be filled *and* emptied by events, to represent a back-and-forth situation. Some events will tick the clock up and some will tick it down.

## Long-term Project

Some projects will take a lot of linnear time. A basic long-term project (like tinkering up a new feature for a device) is eight segments. Truly long-term projects (like creating a new designer drug) can be two, three, or even four clocks, representing all the phases of development, testing, and final completion. Add or subtract clocks depending on the details of the situation and complexity of the project.

A long-term project is a good catch-all for dealing with any unusual player goal, including things that circumvent or change elements of the mechanics or the setting.

One extremely common example of a long-term project clock is learning a skill. Most long-term project clocks are ticked by taking downtime actions whilst killing time.

# Action Roll

When a player character does something challenging, we make an **action roll** to see how it turns out. An action is challenging if there's an obstacle to the PC's goal that's dangerous or troublesome in some way. We don't make an action roll unless the PC is put to the test. If their action is something that we'd expect them to simply accomplish, then we don't make an action roll.

*Each game group will have their own ideas about what "challenging" means. This is good! It's something that establishes the tone and style of your Blades series.*

To make an action roll, we go through six steps. In play, they flow together somewhat, but let's break each one down here for clarity.

* The player states their **goal** for the action.
* The player chooses the **action rating**.
* The GM sets the **position** for the roll.
* The GM sets the **effect level** for the action.
* Add **bonus dice**.
* The player rolls the dice and we judge the result.

## 1. The Player States Their Goal

Your goal is the concrete outcome your character will achieve when they overcome the obstacle at hand. Usually the character's goal is pretty obvious in context, but it's the GM's job to ask and clarify the goal when necessary.

*"You're punching him in the face, right? Okay... what do want to get out of this? Do you want to take him out, or just rough him up so he'll do what you want?"*

## 2. The Player Chooses the Action Rating

The player chooses which **action rating** to roll, following from what their character is doing on-screen. If you want to roll your **Fight** action, then get in a fight. If you want to roll your **Command** action, then order someone around. You can't roll a given action rating unless your character is presently performing that action in the fiction.

## 3. The GM Sets the Position

Once the player chooses their action, the GM sets the **position** for the roll. The position represents how dangerous or troublesome the action might be. There are three positions: **controlled**, **risky**, and **desperate**. To choose a position, the GM looks at the profiles for the positions below and picks one that most closely matches the situation at hand.

**By default, an action roll is risky.** You wouldn't be rolling if there was no risk involved. If the situation seems more dangerous, make it desperate. If it seems less dangerous, make it controlled.

**If you lack the necessary skill for a roll this restricts your position**. Usually ensuring a desperate position.

## 4. The GM Sets the Effect Level

The GM assesses the likely **effect level** of this action, given the factors of the situation. Essentially, the effect level tells us "how much" this action can accomplish: will it have **limited**, **standard**, or **great** effect?

**If you lack the necessary skill for a roll this restricts your effect**. Usually ensuring no more than limited effect.

*The GM's choices for effect level and position can be strongly influenced by the player's choice of action rating. If a player wants to try to make a new friend by **Fighting** someone—well... maybe that's possible, but the GM wouldn't be crazy to say it's a desperate roll and probably limited effect. Seems like **Consorting** would be a lot better for that. The players are always free to choose the action they perform, but that doesn't mean all actions should be equally risky or potent.*

## 5. Add Bonus Dice

You can normally get two bonus dice for your action roll (some special abilities might give you additional bonus dice).

For a bonus die, you can either **Declare a Beneficial Situation** (take 2 paradox) or you can accept a **Devil's Bargain** (you can get dice for both).

### The Devil's Bargain

PCs in *Blades in the Past* can be reckless. To reflect this, the GM or any other player can offer you a bonus die if you accept a Devil's Bargain. Common Devil's Bargains include:

* Collateral damage, unintended harm.
* Betray a friend or loved one.
* Start and/or tick a troublesome clock.
* Add **heat** to the crew from evidence or witnesses.
* Suffer paradox.
* Suffer alienation.
* Become entangled.
* Suffer harm.

The Devil's Bargain occurs regardless of the outcome of the roll. You make the deal, pay the price, and get the bonus die.

The Devil's Bargain is always a free choice. If you don't like one, just reject it (or suggest how to alter it so you might consider taking it). You can always just push yourself for that bonus die instead.

If it's ever needed, the GM has final say over which Devil's Bargains are valid.

## 6. Roll the Dice and Judge the Result

Once the goal, action rating, position, and effect have been established, add any bonus dice and roll the dice pool to determine the outcome. (See the sets of possible outcomes, by position, in the next sections.)

The action roll does a lot of work for you. It tells you how well the character performs as well as how serious the consequences are for them. They might succeed at their action without any consequences (on a **6**), or they might succeed but suffer consequences (on a **4/5**), or it might just all go wrong (on a **1-3**).

On a **1-3**, it's up to the GM to decide if the PC's action has any effect or not, or if it even happens at all. Usually, the action just fails completely, but in some circumstances, it might make sense or be more interesting for the action to have some effect even on a **1-3** result.

Each **4/5** and **1-3** outcome lists suggested **consequences** for the character. The worse your position, the worse the consequences are. The GM can inflict one or more of these consequences, depending on the circumstances of the action roll. PCs have the ability to avoid or reduce the severity of consequences that they suffer by **resisting** them.

When you narrate the action after the roll, the GM and player collaborate together to say what happens on-screen.

## Action Roll Summary

* A player or GM calls for a roll. Make an **action roll** when the character performs a dangerous or troublesome action.
* The player chooses the **action rating** to roll. Choose the action that matches what the character is doing in the fiction.
* The GM establishes the **position** and **effect level** of the action. The choice of position and effect is influenced strongly by the player's choice of action and the presence of an apropriate skill.
* Add up to two bonus dice. 1) **Beneficial Situation** from time travel. 2) a **Devil's Bargain**.
* Roll the dice pool and judge the outcome. The players and GM narrate the action together. The GM has final say over what happens and inflicts consequences as called for by the position and the result of the roll.

#### CONTROLLED

*You act on your terms. You exploit a dominant advantage.*

* **Critical**: You do it with **increased effect**.
* **6**: You do it.
* **4/5**: You hesitate.
    - Withdraw and try a different approach, or
    - else do it with a minor consequence: a **minor complication** occurs, you have **reduced effect**, you suffer **lesser harm**, you end up in a **risky** position.
* **1-3**: You falter.
    - Press on by seizing a **risky** opportunity, or
    - withdraw and try a different approach.

#### RISKY

*You go head to head. You act under fire. You take a chance.*

* **Critical**: You do it with **increased effect**.
* **6**: You do it.
* **4/5**: You do it, but there's a consequence: you suffer **harm**, a **complication** occurs, you have **reduced effect**, you end up in a **desperate** position.
* **1-3**: Things go badly. You suffer **harm**, a **complication** occurs, you end up in a **desperate** position, you **lose this opportunity**.

#### DESPERATE

*You overreach your capabilities. You're in serious trouble.*

* **Critical**: You do it with **increased effect**.
* **6**: You do it.
* **4/5**: You do it, but there's a consequence: you suffer **severe harm**, a **serious complication** occurs, you have **reduced effect**.
* **1-3**: It's the worst outcome. You suffer **severe harm**, a **serious complication** occurs, you **lose this opportunity** for action.

## Double-duty Rolls

Since NPCs don't roll for their actions, an action roll does double-duty: **it resolves the action of the PC as well as any NPCs that are involved**. The single roll tells us how those actions interact and which consequences result. On a **6**, the PC wins and has their effect. On a **4/5**, it's a mix—both the PC and the NPC have their effect. On a **1-3**, the NPC wins and has their effect as a consequence on the PC.

# Effect

In *Blades in the Past*, you achieve goals by taking actions and facing consequences. But how many actions does it take to achieve a particular goal? That depends on the **effect level** of your actions. The GM judges the effect level using the profiles below. Which one best matches the action at hand -- **great**, **standard**, or **limited**? Each effect level indicates the questions that should be answered for that effect, as well as how many segments to tick if you're using a **progress clock**.

| Effect Levels |                                                                                                                  | Ticks |
| ------------: | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---: |
|     **GREAT** | *You achieve more than usual. How does the extra effort manifest? What additional benefit do you enjoy?*         |   3   |
|  **STANDARD** | *You achieve what we'd expect as "normal" with this action. Is that enough, or is there more left to do?*        |   2   |
|   **LIMITED** | *You achieve a partial or weak effect. How is your impact diminished? What effort remains to achieve your goal?* |   1   |

## Assessing Factors

To assess effect level, first start with your gut feeling, given this situation. Then, if needed, assess three factors that may modify the effect level: **potency**, **scale**, and **quality**. If the PC has an advantage in a given factor, consider a higher effect level. If they have a disadvantage, consider a reduced effect level.

### Potency

The potency factor considers particular weaknesses, taking extra time or a bigger risk, or the influence of arcane powers. An infiltrator is more potent if all the lights are extinguished and they move about in the dark.

### Quality/Tier

Quality represents the effectiveness of tools, weapons, or other resources.

### Scale

Scale represents the number of opponents, size of an area covered, scope of influence, etc. Larger scale can be an advantage or disadvantage depending on the situation. In battle, more people are better. When infiltrating, more people are a hindrance.

When considering factors, effect level might be reduced below limited, resulting in **zero effect**—or increased beyond great, resulting in an **extreme effect**.

If a PC special ability gives "+1 effect," it comes into play *after* the GM has assessed the effect level.

Also, remember that a jumper can get an **advantageous situation** (take 2 paradox) to get +1 effect on their action.

Every factor won't always apply to every situation. You don't have to do an exact accounting every time, either. Use the factors to help you make a stronger judgment call—don't feel beholden to them.

## Trading Position for Effect

After factors are considered and the GM has announced the effect level, a player might want to trade position for effect, or vice versa. For instance, if they're going to make a risky roll with standard effect (the most common scenario, generally), they might instead want to push their luck and make a desperate roll but with great effect.

This kind of trade-off isn't included in the effect factors because it's not an element the GM should assess when setting the effect level. Once the level is set, though, you can always offer the trade-off to the player if it makes sense in the situation.

## Consequences

When a PC suffers an effect from an enemy or a dangerous situation, it's called a **consequence**. Consequences are the companion to effects. PCs have effect on the world around them and they suffer consequences in return from the risks they face.

# Setting Position & Effect

The GM sets position and effect for an action roll at the same time, after the player says what they're doing and chooses their action. Usually, **Risky / Standard** is the default combination, modified by the action being used, the strength of the opposition, and the effect factors.

The ability to set position and effect as independent variables gives you nine combinations to choose from, to help you convey a wide array of fictional circumstances.

# Consequences and Harm

Enemy actions, bad circumstances, or the outcome of a roll can inflict **consequences** on a PC. There are five types.

A given circumstance might result in one or more consequences, depending on the situation. The GM determines the consequences, following from the fiction and the style and tone established by the game group.

## Reduced Effect

This consequence represents impaired performance. The PC's action isn't as effective as they'd anticipated. You hit him, but it's only a flesh wound. She accepts the forged invitation, but she'll keep her eye on you throughout the night. You're able to scale the wall, but it's slow going—you're only halfway up. This consequence essentially reduces the effect level of the PC's action by one after all other factors are accounted for.

## Complication

This consequence represents trouble, mounting danger, or a new threat. The GM might introduce an immediate problem that results from the action right now: the room catches fire, you're disarmed, the circle takes +1 **heat** from evidence or witnesses, the target evades you and now it's a chase, reinforcements arrive, etc.

Or the GM might tick a clock for the complication, instead. Maybe there's a clock for the alert level of the guards at the museum. Or maybe the GM creates a new clock for the suspicion of the noble guests at the masquerade party and ticks it. Fill one tick on a clock for a minor complication or two ticks for a standard complication.

A **serious complication** is more severe: reinforcements surround and trap you, the room catches fire and falling ceiling beams block the door, your weapon is broken, the circle suffers +2 **heat**, your target escapes out of sight, etc. Fill three ticks on a clock for a serious complication.

**Don't inflict a complication that negates a successful roll.** If a PC tries to corner an enemy and gets a **4/5**, don't say that the enemy escapes. The player's roll succeeded, so the enemy is cornered... maybe the PC has to wrestle them into position and during the scuffle the enemy grabs their gun.

## Lost Opportunity

This consequence represents shifting circumstance. You had an opportunity to achieve your goal with this action, but it slips away. To try again, you need a new approach—usually a new form of action or a change in circumstances. Maybe you tried to **Fight** with the noble to trap her on the balcony, but she evades your maneuver and leaps out of reach. If you want to trap her now you'll have to try another way—maybe by **Swaying** her with your roguish charm.

## Worse Position

This consequence represents losing control of the situation—the action carries you into a more dangerous position. You haven't failed, but you haven't succeeded yet, either. You can try again, re-rolling at the new, worse position. This is a good consequence to choose to show escalating action. A situation might go from controlled, to risky, to desperate as the action plays out and the PC gets deeper and deeper in trouble.

## Harm

This consequence represents a long-lasting debility (or death). When you suffer harm, record the specific injury on your character sheet equal to the level of harm you suffer. If you suffer **lesser harm**, record it in the bottom row. If you suffer **moderate harm**, write it in the middle row. If you suffer **severe harm**, record it in the top row. See examples of harm and the harm tracker, below.

Your character suffers the penalty indicated at the end of the row if any or all harm recorded in that row applies to the situation at hand. So, if you have "Drained" and "Battered" harm in the bottom row, you'll suffer reduced effect when you try to run away from the constables. When you're impaired by harm in the top row (severe harm, level 3), your character is incapacitated and can't do anything directly, but can still spend paradox to create advantageous situations, declare details, etc...

If you need to mark a harm level, but the row is already filled, the harm moves up to the next row above. So, if you suffered standard harm (level 2) but had no empty spaces in the second row, you'd have to record severe harm (level 3), instead. If you run out of spaces on the top row and need to mark harm there, your character suffers a **catastrophic, permanent consequence** (loss of a limb, sudden death, etc., depending on the circumstances).

Remember you can almost always jump away before this happens. Lick your wound. Heal up. Come at it from another angle.

# Avoidance and Armour

When your PC suffers a consequence that you don't like, you can choose to declare a situation which allows you to avoid it. Just tell the GM, "No, I don't think so. I'm avoiding that", and then explain what unexpected occurence protects you from the consequence. Avoidance is always automatically effective—the GM will tell you if the consequence is reduced in severity or if you avoid it entirely. Then, you'll make an **avoiance roll** to see how much paradox your character suffers as a result of their avoidance.

You make the roll using one of your character's **attributes** (**Mind**, **Body**, or **Self**). The GM chooses the attribute, based on the nature of consequences:

* **Mind**: Consequences from deception or understanding.
* **Body**: Consequences from physical strain or injury.
* **Self**: Consequences from mental strain or willpower.

Your character suffers **6 paradox** when they avoid the consquence, **minus the highest die result from the avoidance roll**. So, if you rolled a **4**, you'd suffer 2 paradox. If you rolled a **6**, you'd suffer zero paradox. On a **critical** you also gain one **grace**. When you do this you need to note down in your **destiny** that you need to set up the situation which saved you ... only by doing so can you remove the paradox.

Usually, an avoidance roll **reduces the severity** of a consequence. If you're going to suffer fatal harm, for example, an avoidance roll would reduce the harm to severe, instead. Or if you got a complication when you were sneaking into the manor house, and the GM was going to mark three ticks on the "Alert" clock, she'd only mark two (or maybe one) if you avoid the complication.

**You may only roll against a given consequence once.**

The GM also has the option to rule that your character **completely avoids** the consequence. For instance, maybe you're in a sword fight and the consequence is getting disarmed. When you avoid it, the GM says that you avoid that consequence completely: you keep hold of your weapon.

**By adjusting which consequences are reduced vs. which are completely avoided, the GM establishes the overall tone of your game**. For a more daring game, most consequences will be completely avoided. For a grittier game, most consequences will only be reduced with avoidance.

The GM may also threaten several consequences at once, then the player may choose which ones to avoid (and make rolls for each).

Once you decide to avoid a consequence and roll, you suffer the paradox indicated. You can't roll first and see how much paradox you'll take, then decide whether or not to avoid it.

## Armour

If you have a type of **armour** that applies to the situation, you can mark an armour box to reduce or avoid a consequence, instead of rolling to avoid.

When an armour box is marked, it can't be used again until it's restored. All of your armour is restored when you declare your **prep** for the next escapade.

## Death

There are a couple ways for a PC to die:

* If they suffer level 4 fatal harm and they don't avoid it, they die. *Sometimes this is a choice a player wants to make, because they feel like it wouldn't make sense for the character to survive or it seems right for their character to die here.*
* If they need to record harm at level 3 and it's already filled, they suffer a catastrophic consequence, which might mean sudden death (depending on the circumstances).

When your character dies, you have options:

* You can create a new scoundrel to play. Maybe you "promote" one of the NPC gang members to a PC, or create a brand new character who joins the crew.

## Destined Death

As a last ditch option a character can avoid death by jumping out and having an older double jump in at the same moment. This places a destiny on the character to return to this exact moment at some point in their personal future and *die*. Activating this option costs no paradox immediately, but if the PC later experiences something which appears to make it impossible to do this then they will immediately receive 6 paradox which can only be cleared by fulfilling this destiny somehow.

This option should always be by player choice, and never imposed by the GM.

# Fortune Roll

The fortune roll is a tool the GM can use to disclaim decision making. You use a fortune roll in two different ways:

**When you need to make a determination about a situation the PCs aren't directly involved in** and don't want to simply decide the outcome.

**When an outcome is uncertain**, but no other roll applies to the situation at hand.

When you make a fortune roll you may assess **any trait rating** to determine the dice pool of the roll.

* When a PC **gathers information**, you might make a fortune roll using their **action rating** to determine the amount of the info they get.

If no trait applies, roll 1d for sheer luck or create a dice pool (from one to four) based on the situation at hand. If two parties are directly opposed, make a fortune roll for each side to see how they do, then assess the outcome of the situation by comparing their performance levels.

The fortune roll is also a good tool to help the GM manage all the various moving parts of the world. Sometimes a quick roll is enough to answer a question or inspire an idea for what might happen next.

* **Critical**: Exceptional result / Great, extreme effect.
* **6**: Good result / Standard,  full effect.

# Gathering Information

The flow of information from the GM to the players about the fictional world is very important in any roleplaying game and even more important in a game about time travel. Information management is a key part of play, to the extent that one of the character playbooks in this game is dedicated to it.

By default, the GM tells the players what their characters perceive, suspect, and intuit. But there's just too much going on to say *everything*—it would take forever and be boring, too. What's more there's plenty of information which is not readily available by default, but which can be discovered through research. The players have a tool at their disposal to more fully investigate the fictional world.

When you want to know something specific about the fictional world, your character can **gather information**. The GM will ask you **how** your character gathers the info.

If it's common knowledge, the GM will simply answer your questions. If there's an obstacle to the discovery of the answer, an action roll is called for. If it's not common knowledge but there's no obstacle, a simple fortune roll determines the quality of the information you gather.

There is a very important question which the GM must judge before every information gathering attempt: what time periods does this information source cover?

Usually most information gathering can only provide information about the **absolute past**, but there is one exception: when the source of information has travelled in time. In that case only their **personal past** is covered.

The most common gather information actions are **Understanding** the situation to reveal or anticipate what's going on, **Studying** records understand what has happened, and **Consorting** with people to extract rumours and personal details.

Sometimes, you'll have to maneuver yourself into position before you can gather information. For example, you might have to **Sneak** into a building first and then **Study** the records you find there.


## Investigation

Some questions are too complex to answer immediately with a single gather information roll. For instance, you might want to discover a time when a particular artifact disappears from the historical record. In these cases, the research becomes a **downtime action**, which can be worked on when **killing time**.

When you begin the investigation create a progress clock. Usually this will be a single 4 segment clock for simple questions, but the more detailed the question and the more hard to determine the information required the larger the clock. It is even possible some investigations may require multiple clocks.

Progressing the clock is a **downtime action** covered in the **downtime** section.

The player may choose to increase the size of the clock to obtain either a greater effect once it is complete or extra dice on the final fortune roll. Every additional 2 segments added to the clock at the start gives either an additional +1 effect or +1d at the end.

## Tracking a Time Jumper

One of the most common uses of the investigation rules is to track a time jumper in a known region of space-time. In this case a 4 segment clock will provide **Limitted effect**. If the time jumper is actively attempting to hide use the opposed investigation role rules above. 

In particular if the jumper spends an extended period killing time in the targeted region or generated any heat whilst there then the information is considered particularly hard to conceal. On the other hand if the jumper goes to ground in their own safehouse in the region then that makes the information particularly easy to conceal.


# Character creation

## Choose a Playbook

The playbooks are shown in the next section in detail, here's a simple summary:

* **Artisan**: A creator and procurer of items and devices
* **Diplomat**: A persuasive speaker at home in social situations
* **Initiate**: A strange being who knows things others don't
* **Trickster**: A scoundrel who gets into trouble whenever they are
* **Oracle**: A master of information flow and control
* **Warrior**: A fighter of battles and specialist in physical force

## Choose your region of origin

Choose the region of space-time where your character originated. Usually this will be roughly contemporary with the region in which the circle is based, but that's not mandatory. Characters from far ahead or behind are also possible, and most playbooks have some options for designing such characters. The Initiate in particular is unlikely to originate from the modern era.

## Assign four action dots

Your playbook begins with three action dots already placed. You get to add four more dots (so you'll have seven total). At the start of the game, no action rating may have more than two dots (unless a special ability tells you otherwise).

## Choose a special ability

Take a look at the special abilities for your playbook and choose one. If you can't decide which one to pick, go with the first one on the list—it's placed there as a good default choice.

### Special Armor

Some special abilities refer to your **special armor**. Each character sheet has a set of three boxes to track usage of armor (standard, heavy, and special). If you have any abilities that use your special armor, tick its box when you activate one of them. If you don't have any special abilities that use special armor, then you can't use that armor box at all.

## Choose one close friend and one rival

Each playbook has a list of notable jumper NPCs that your character knows. Choose one from the list who is a close relationship (a good friend, a lover, a family relation, etc.). Mark the upward-pointing triangle next to their name. Then choose another NPC on the list who's your rival or enemy. Mark the downward-pointing triangle next to their name.

## Choose your support

Your support is a place and time at which you have a normal linnear life. Spending time in that life living like other people do will help you stave off **alienation** and heal from some forms of **harm**. Each playbook has some examples of reasonable supports for the character, although you can come up with others. The main important thing is that:

1. Your support *must* be a non-jumper life
2. Your support *must* be located in a particular region of space-time, it can't be something you can access wherever you are
3. Your support *must* include some NPC or NPCs who will notice and cause complications if you suddenly turn up years older after only a day away

## Record your name and look

Choose a name for your character. Record a few evocative words that describe your character's look.

# Circle Creation

## Choose a Fraternal Order

There are a number of fraternal orders which exist within the society to specialise in particular activities considered beneficial to all jumpers. The following is not an exclusive list, but covers the most major ones which PCs might wish to be affiliated with:

* **Curators**: track and manage historically important items, often saving them from desctruction and keeping them in their bases like cross-temporal museums
* **Couriers**: Specialise in carrying people, things, and information to when they need to be
* **Hunters**: Seek out and hunt rogue jumpers and the incursions by time travellers from outside the purvew of the society
* **Surgeons**: Specialise in the medical complications of time travel, especially the spread of disease between eras due to careless activity
* **Continuity**: Step in when an historical fact needs to be maintained, even if it means making it happen themselves

## Establish your region

Every circle operates out of a main circle house, a base from which escapades can be planned and activities can be managed. By default every circle house has multiple rooms with locking doors which can be used to ensure that the residents don't cross their own timelines more than is absolutely necessary. A circle house also has a duration as well as a location: it is established at a particular moment, and disestablished at another. In some cases the disestablishment is more of a formality -- handing over to a different circle which uses the same building in a different time period. Other times it means fully cleaning and stripping the facility of everything related to jumping or even arranging for it to be destroyed outright.

Decomissioning their own circle-house is a task which might be given to members of the circle early in their career. Establishing it to begin with is much more likely to be handled exclusively by late-career high-rank versions of the members.

As a result the state of the circle house when the characters are first inducted into it may well give them clues about their own future escapades. This can be used to provide foreshaddowing, or to set up **destiny** which must be fulfilled.

As well as the circle house itself the circle will also be responsible for a surrounding region of space-time. Jumpers arriving in that region without prior arrangement are the circle's responsibility to deal with. Usually these will be simply travellers passing through eager to get news and somewhere to recharge safely. Other times they may be something else: rogues, sollipsists, or even intruders from beyond history. Sometimes they may even be potential new circle members who for whatever reason didn't get along with the circle they were originally inducted into.

> The typical region of control for a circle in industrial times is a single named settlement such as a small town and surrounding country, or a neighbourhood in a larger city, and usually covering only a period of around 50 years. Some circles may have larger or smaller areas or durations of control depending on how historically dense this part of space-time is. 

### Establish your neighbours

It's worth jotting down some details about neighbouring circles you may interact with. These can be adjacent in time or space, or both.

* Who was in your circle-house before you? Or was it created or adapted from a normal building at the start of your tenure? If so where was the circle house for the previous circle that covered this area located, and why wasn't it continued?
* Who will be in your circle-house after you? Or will it be destroyed or decomissioned? If so where will the new circle house be located, and why will it be moved?
* Which circles cover adjacent regions in space? Do they have the same duration as you, or do they partially overlap?

For each neighbour circle it's fine to just think about which Fraternal Order they pledge to, and whether you are friendly or hostile to them.

## Choose a special ability

Take a look at the special abilities for your circle and choose one. If you can't decide which one to pick, go with the first one on the list—it's placed there as a good default choice. It's important to pick a special ability that everyone is excited about.

Just like picking the Fraternal order type and region choosing a special ability is another chance to focus the game down to a more specific range of possibilities. That's a lot to work with, and it helps get the game going in a strong direction from the very beginning.

## Assign circle upgrades

An upgrade is a valuable asset that helps the circle in some way. Each circle type has two pre-selected **upgrades** that suit that circle.

You get to add **two additional upgrades** to your new circle (so you'll have a total of four upgrades when you start). You can choose from the specific upgrades available to your Fraternal order or the general upgrades on the circle sheet.

## Choose a Mentor

Every circle has a mentor, a higher rank individual who is managing this circle for the fraternal order. One of their main responsibilities is making sure that low rank jumpers learn and become higher rank jumpers, so they will often be unwilling to intervene in escapades, especially if they already know the outcome in their own personal past. Each Fraternal Order has a list of four potential mentors, choose the one you feel works best for your circle.

There are a number of circle upgrades which can interact with and alter your mentor, either granting additional capabilities to them or making them more likely to intervene in specific situations. In particular note the **Destined Mentor** upgrade, which allows you to select one of the PCs and write becoming the mentor into their destiny.

**Unless an upgrade specified otherwise all mentors are Rank 3 jumpers and will not intervene in escapades other than to help transport the circle to and from the region where the escapade is expected to happen**

## Look at the circle's destiny

Every circle sheet contains a destiny track. The events on this destiny track can be seen evidently by the PCs: the effects of these on the circle house are visible from the first time they enter the house. However someone is going to have to take the actions needed to ensure these things happen as they are destined, and that task will fall upon the PCs at some point in their career.

The track also indicates what the maximum rank any character in the circle may have is. The actual process for rising in rank is covered in the **downtime actions** section.
